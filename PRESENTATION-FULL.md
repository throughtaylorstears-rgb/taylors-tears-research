### Slide 3 — The Setup

August 6-7, 2026. Wyndham Executive Center, Columbia MO.

I'm in prison. There's a program called StoryLink. Once a month, they bring hundreds of books into the women's facility. If you haven't had any violations, you get to choose a book, record yourself reading it on a CD, and they ship it to your child. Free. No strings.

I chose Clifford. I chose Dr. Seuss. I chose anything that made my daughter — who was two years old when I went in — feel like I was still there.

---

### Slide 4 — The Moment

I wrote "When Addiman Came" in there. But I didn't write it because I wanted to be an author. I wrote it because I knew something:

**Every kid in that prison — every single one — has someone on the outside who's dealing with addiction. And every single one of those kids deserves a resource that explains what's happening in a way THAT CHILD can understand.**

I wrote what I wished existed for my kid.

# PART 2: THE CHILD'S REALITY
## What Kids Actually Experience

---

### Slide 5 — Her Words

My daughter's ten now. She's old enough to have real conversations. But when she was two, three, four — during active addiction — her words were simple:

**"Mommy, I want you."**

That's it. That's the whole sentence. That's all she knew how to say.

And somewhere, some caseworker, some counselor, some well-meaning relative told her: "Your mom's on drugs" or "your mom's in trouble" or "your mom chose drugs over you."

---

### Slide 6 — What Kids Sense

Here's what professionals need to understand:

**Children sense crisis before adults acknowledge it.**

They feel the tension in the house before anyone says anything. They notice when the lights go out at 2 PM. They hear the arguments. They see the needle on the counter. They know.

But adults? We think we're hiding it. We think kids don't notice. We're wrong.

---

### Slide 7 — Internalization

Kids don't process crisis like adults do. When a parent is in addiction, the child doesn't think "my parent is sick."

**They think: "What's wrong with ME?"**

- "Did I do something bad?"
- "If I were better, maybe they'd stop."
- "Maybe I'm just not worth staying for."

This is what silence and vague reassurance does. It leaves a void — and kids fill that void with self-blame.

---

### Slide 8 — The Hardest Moment

[PAUSE. Let the room get quiet.]

I need to tell you about the day I walked out on my daughter.

She was about three. I was withdrawing — sick, miserable, barely functional. Her dad took her to the store. She came back with chocolate.

She said: **"Mommy, I brought chocolate to make you feel better."**

I said nothing will make me feel better.

I got up. I walked out.

She screamed. She beat on the other side of the door. She yelled, "Mommy!"

I still hear that. Every single day.

---

### Slide 9 — The Question

That moment? That's what we're trying to prevent. That's why we're here.

Not just crisis response. Not just stabilization. We're talking about the ripple effects — the moment a child learns that love isn't enough to make the pain stop.

---

# PART 3: THE LANGUAGE THAT HARMS
## Words That Close Doors

---

### Slide 10 — Read These

I'm going to read you some things that were said to me. And I'm going to ask you to listen — really listen — to how they land.

Let's start simple:

> **"Why don't you just stop?"**

That sounds simple. But it tells you they don't understand addiction at all. It turns a crisis into a character flaw.

---

### Slide 11

> **"You're choosing this over your daughter."**

Sometimes people say this thinking it will wake someone up. Usually it piles shame on top of pain.

**Shame is gasoline for addiction.**

---

### Slide 12

> **"If you really loved her, you'd get clean."**

This confuses love with functioning. A parent in active addiction can deeply love their child and still be very sick.

---

### Slide 13

> **"Your daughter would be better off without you."**

That one can be devastating. For someone already drowning in guilt, it can push them further into using, disappearing, or giving up entirely.

---

### Slide 14

> **"You're a bad mom."**

That kind of statement can stick in someone's head for years. It does nothing to create safety, honesty, or change.

---

### Slide 15 — The Pattern

**The worst ones attack identity, not behavior.**

They make you feel like you are broken beyond repair — instead of someone struggling, overwhelmed, traumatized, and in need of real support.

---

# PART 4: THE LANGUAGE THAT HEALS
## Words That Open Doors

---

### Slide 16 — What Would Have Helped

What if instead of all that, someone had said:

> **"I know you love your daughter."**

---

### Slide 17

> **"You're not beyond help."**

---

### Slide 18

> **"Let's figure out what's making this so hard."**

---

### Slide 19

> **"You do not have to fix everything today."**

---

### Slide 20

> **"Your daughter needs your healing, not your shame."**

---

### Slide 21 — The Difference

The difference is this:

**The harmful language attacks WHO YOU ARE.**

**The healing language addresses WHAT'S HAPPENING.**

One says: "You're a bad person."
The other says: "This is hard, and I see you in it."

---

### Slide 22 — What Actually Helped

There was an officer who arrested me once. When I got out of prison, he said:

> **"When you get out this time, move to a new place. You can be whoever you want to be. You can make new friends, start a job where no one knows your history."**

That's it. That's all he said.

But it gave me hope. It told me I wasn't defined by my record. It planted a seed.

That — more than any treatment program, more than any therapy — is what made me believe I could change.

# PART 5: STORY-BASED TOOLS
## Metaphors That Work

---

### Slide 23 — Why Metaphors Work

Kids don't understand clinical language. They don't know what "substance use disorder" means. They don't understand "relapse" or "harm reduction" or "trauma response."

But they DO understand story.

**Storytelling and metaphor help children make sense of chaos.**

---

### Slide 24 — Addiman

In "When Addiman Came," I personify addiction as a character — called "Addiman." He's not a scary monster. He's more like a fog, a shadow, a presence that sneaks in when nobody's looking.

Why?

Because when you give addiction a form that's not terrifying, you give kids permission to talk about it.

---

### Slide 25 — Bloo's Big Feelings

My second book is "Bloo's Big Feelings." It's about a blue bunny who meets different emotions — anger, sadness, fear, frustration, embarrassment, joy.

Each emotion has an animal guide:
- Bear for anger
- Deer for sadness
- Owl for fear
- Beaver for frustration

Why? Because kids connect with animals. Animals don't judge. Animals just... help.

---

[READ A PAGE FROM BLOO'S BIG FEELINGS — Anger section]

### Slide 27 — What This Does

This isn't just cute. This is trauma-informed care at its simplest:

1. **Names the feeling** — kids learn emotions have words
2. **Gives a strategy** — breathing, grounding, finding helpers
3. **Validates** — "This feeling is here, but it won't stay"
4. **Models connection** — the animal guide shows help exists

# PART 6: PRACTICAL APPLICATION
## What Professionals Can Do Tomorrow

---

### Slide 28 — Three Things

Here are three things you can do starting tomorrow:

**1. Check your language.**
Are you adding shame, or reducing it? Before you speak, ask: "Would I say this to my own child?"

**2. Use a metaphor.**
Instead of "your parent's sick," try: "Your parent has a tricky visitor called Addiman. It's not your fault."

**3. Include the child.**
Don't talk ABOUT the child. Talk TO the child. Ask them what THEY feel. They're not invisible.

---

### Slide 29 — Scenario: Child Asks About Their Parent

**[PLAY/ACT OUT]**

A kid in your care looks up and asks: **"When is my mom coming back?"**

**WHAT NOT TO SAY:**
- "She's not coming back."
- "Your mom's in trouble."
- "I don't know."

**WHAT TO SAY INSTEAD:**

> "I don't know when she'll be back. But I DO know that you are loved, and you are safe right now."

Or:

> "I can't answer that, but I can tell you: you didn't cause this, and it's not your job to fix it."

---

### Slide 29B — Scenario: Parent Refuses Services

A parent stands up, grabs their coat, says **"I'm done. I'm leaving."**

**WHAT NOT TO SAY:**
- "You can't leave."
- "If you leave, you're proving you don't care."
- "Fine, but you'll regret this."

**WHAT TO SAY INSTEAD:**

> "I hear you. This is hard, and I know you didn't ask to be here. Can you tell me what's making you want to leave?"

Or:

> "You're allowed to leave. But I want you to know: when you're ready, we'll be here. No judgment."

**WHY IT WORKS:** Giving them control back reduces resistance. They leave feeling respected — and they often come back.

---

### Slide 29C — Scenario: Parent Comes In intoxicated

A parent arrives at your facility, clearly intoxicated, slurred speech, unsteady on their feet.

**WHAT NOT TO SAY:**
- "You're drunk. Come back when you're sober."
- "I can't talk to you like this."
- "You're wasting my time."

**WHAT TO SAY INSTEAD:**

> "I can see you're going through something right now. Let's just sit together for a minute."

Or:

> "I'm glad you're here. Let's get you some water. We can talk when you're ready."

**WHY IT WORKS:** Meeting them where they are. Intoxication isn't the end — it's often the beginning of a crisis conversation.

---

### Slide 29D — Scenario: Kid Has a Meltdown in the Hallway

A child in your care suddenly starts crying loudly, maybe hitting, screaming, "I want my mom!"

**WHAT NOT TO SAY:**
- "Stop crying."
- "You're fine."
- "Be quiet — you're embarrassing yourself."

**WHAT TO SAY INSTEAD:**

> "You seem really upset. I'm right here. You're safe."

Or (get on their level):

> "I see big feelings are here. That's okay. Let's breathe together. In... out..."

**WHY IT WORKS:** Naming the feeling ("upset," "big feelings") normalizes it. Getting on their level shows you're with them, not against them.

---

### Slide 30 — The Framework

Here's the simple framework for ALL scenarios:

1. **Acknowledge** — "I hear you" / "I see you"
2. **Validate** — "That makes sense" / "Your feelings are okay"
3. **Redirect** — "Right now, here's what we have"
4. **Protect** — "You are safe"
